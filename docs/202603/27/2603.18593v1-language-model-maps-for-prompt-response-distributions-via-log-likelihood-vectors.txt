Abstract 

We propose a method that represents language models by log-likelihood vectors over prompt-response pairs and constructs model maps for comparing their conditional distributions. In this space, distances between models approx-imate the KL divergence between the corre-sponding conditional distributions. Experi-ments on a large collection of publicly available language models show that the maps capture meaningful global structure, including relation-ships to model attributes and task performance. The method also captures systematic shifts in-duced by prompt modifications and their ap-proximate additive compositionality, suggest-ing a way to analyze and predict the effects of composite prompt operations. We further introduce pointwise mutual information (PMI) vectors to reduce the influence of unconditional distributions; in some cases, PMI-based model maps better reflect training-data-related differ-ences. Overall, the framework supports the analysis of input-dependent model behavior. 

1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) are becoming in-creasingly diverse, creating a growing need for frameworks that systematically compare the be-haviors of different models (Zhu et al., 2025; Yax et al., 2025; Zhuang et al., 2025; Horwitz et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025). Quantifying distances between models provides a foundation for a wide range of applications, including model selection, performance prediction, and the analysis of distil-lation and evolution. A log-likelihood vector (Oyama et al., 2025b) is a feature representation that characterizes a lan-guage model by its log-likelihoods over a prede-fined set of texts. 2 Related Work 

Existing studies comparing a large number of language models can be broadly categorized by the type of information they use into approaches based on model internals, generated texts, and log-likelihood vectors. 

Comparison based on model internals. Com-parisons based on model weights and intermediate representations can directly examine similarities in internal structure. For example, Zhu et al. (2025) analyzed relationships among models based on sta-tistical similarities of their parameters, and Horwitz et al. (2025) compared the weights of many mod-els on Hugging Face to reveal relationships that are not easily visible from publicly available in-formation alone. In addition, Zhou et al. (2025) measured similarities among models using changes in activations induced by linguistic minimal pairs. While these approaches are useful for analyzing model internals, it is not straightforward to provide a common basis for comparison across models with different architectures or representation spaces. 

Comparison based on generated texts. Com-parisons based on generated texts can directly ad-dress the practical setting of conditional generation. Jiang et al. (2025) mapped generated responses into a text embedding space and measured differences among models based on distances in that space. In addition, Yax et al. (2025) introduced a similar-ity measure based on conditional probabilities and estimated phylogenetic distances among language models. However, such approaches can be affected by the randomness of the generation process and by the choice of embedding-based representations for generated outputs. 

Comparison based on log-likelihood vectors. 

Methods that represent models as vectors based on their log-likelihoods on a pre-defined set of texts (Oyama et al., 2025b,a; Kishino et al., 2026) are useful in that they enable many models to be compared in a shared space without relying on generation, and they also allow distances between vectors to be interpreted in relation to the KL diver-gence between probability distributions. However, existing methods mainly focus on unconditional distributions and do not directly address the setting of conditional generation. Our study aims to fill this gap. Specifically, we introduce comparisons based on conditional log-likelihood vectors and use PMI-vector repre-sentations that subtract unconditional components, thereby providing a framework for comparing the conditional probability distributions of many lan-3guage models.

54.2 Selection of language models 

To construct the model maps, we considered text generation models listed on Open LLM Leader-board v2. Among them, we restricted our analysis to models with at least 1,000 downloads and an available chat_template . We also restricted the model size to no larger than 70B due to computa-tional resource constraints. In addition, for each model, we attempted to compute log-likelihoods and retained only those for which the computation was numerically stable, without overflow or related issues. The resulting number of models differs across experiments; specifically, we use K = 552 

models for the conditional model map and prompt-shift analyses, and K = 481 models for the PMI model map analysis. 

4.3 Computation of the log-likelihood 

For each language model, we computed the con-ditional log-likelihood log p(y|x) and the uncon-ditional log-likelihood log p(y) for each text pair 

(x, y ). To compute log p(y|x), we fed each prompt-response pair into the model using the correspond-ing chat_template . To compute log p(y), we used the same chat_template with the user in-put left empty, and computed the log-likelihood of the response. All computations were performed using bfloat16 precision. In addition, to reduce the influence of extremely small log-likelihood values, we applied clipping separately to log p(y|x) and 

log p(y) using the lower 2% threshold computed from all model-text combinations.

5.2 Prompt shift 

Unlike Oyama et al. (2025b), which focuses on unconditional distributions, the use of conditional log-likelihood vectors allows us to directly examine how the generation distribution of each language model changes on the model map in response to changes in the input prompt. Let T (x) denote an operation that transforms an input x. In our frame-work, p(·| x) and p(·| T (x)) can be compared in the same space as distinct conditional probability mod-els. 5.3 Additive compositionality of prompt shift 

Figure 2 visualizes the conditional log-likelihood vectors under these four settings. Specifically, for each model, we project the vectors onto the lin-ear subspace spanned by the mean shift vectors 

¯vcot and ¯vrep , and then apply PCA within that sub-space to obtain a two-dimensional visualization. The black points represent the BASE models shown with labels, while other BASE models are shown in gray without labels in the background. The red, blue, and yellow points represent COT , REPEAT ,and REPEAT +COT , respectively, and are connected by line segments to the corresponding BASE mod-els. This figure shows systematic position changes in response to the prompt transformations, indicat-ing prompt shift. From the positional relationships among these points, an additive compositionality of the shifts in-duced by COT and REPEAT is suggested, analogous to the additive compositionality observed in word embeddings (Mikolov et al., 2013). That is, 

vrep+cot ,i ≈ vcot ,i + vrep ,i (13) holds. Indeed, the median and mean of the relative prediction error 

∥vcot ,i + vrep ,i − vrep+cot ,i ∥/∥vrep+cot ,i + ci∥

were 0.062 and 0.081, respectively, indicating small errors 5. When ℓ is replaced by ξ, the cor-responding median and mean are 0.074 and 0.095. Moreover, additive compositionality is also ob-served in the mean conditional log-likelihood ¯ℓi.Specifically, let δcot , δrep , and δrep+cot denote the changes in ¯ℓi from BASE to COT , REPEAT , and 

REPEAT +COT , respectively. Then, the Pearson cor-relation coefficient between δrep+cot and δcot +δrep 

was 0.98. These results suggest that the effects of prompt transformations are composed approximately ad-ditively in the conditional log-likelihood vector space 6. This additive compositionality suggests the possibility of predicting the effect of a com-posite prompt operation as the sum of the shifts induced by its individual operations. Furthermore, by transferring a prompt-shift vector obtained from one model to another, it may be possible to predict the effect of an unobserved prompt transformation, as well as the resulting change in task performance.

7 Conclusion 

We proposed a framework for constructing model maps for prompt-response distributions using con-ditional log-likelihood vectors. In this shared space, distances between models approximate KL diver-gence between conditional distributions, extending previous log-likelihood-vector-based model maps to conditional settings. We also introduced PMI vectors to reduce the contribution of unconditional distributions and examined their relation to training-data-dependent differences. We further showed that prompt modifications induce systematic shifts in model position and that these shifts exhibit approx-imate additive compositionality. These results sug-gest that the proposed framework is useful for com-paring conditional model behavior across language models and analyzing prompt transformations in a common space. 8Limitations 

• This study focuses on models whose weights are publicly available, and extending the method to closed models remains future work. • The experiments are conducted mainly on instruction-style prompt-response datasets such as Tulu3 and Infinity-Instruct. It remains unclear to what extent the proposed frame-work generalizes to other settings, such as non-instructional text, multilingual data, code generation, or longer and more complex inter-actions. • The effectiveness of PMI-based quantities is not uniform across all analyses. Although PMI vectors can in some cases reflect training-data-related differences more clearly than con-ditional log-likelihood vectors, this tendency is not consistently observed, and the practi-cal advantage of PMI-based model maps may depend on the dataset and models being com-pared. • The prompt-shift analysis is limited to a small number of prompt transformations, namely appending a CoT suffix and repeating the prompt. It remains an open question whether similar geometric regularities and approxi-mate additive compositionality hold for a broader class of prompt edits. • The visualizations in this paper rely on low-dimensional projections such as t-SNE and PCA. While these are useful for qualitative inspection, they do not fully preserve the ge-ometry of the original high-dimensional space, so apparent proximity or clustering in two di-mensions should be interpreted with caution. • The empirical validation in Section 6 is con-ducted on a limited set of eight language mod-els. Although the observed correlations sup-port the proposed approximation, the robust-ness of these findings across a broader range of models, decoding settings, and evaluation prompts remains to be investigated. • The proposed framework depends on specific formatting choices for prompt-response pairs, including prompt templates and prompt trans-formations. Such implementation details may affect the resulting log-likelihood vectors and model positions, and disentangling these ef-fects from more intrinsic behavioral differ-ences remains future work. • The KL-divergence approximation in (7) as-sumes that both pi(y|x) and pj (y|x) are suf-ficiently close to p0(y|x). If this assumption does not hold, the estimated KL divergence may be substantially inaccurate. • Each component ∆ℓis of the PMI vector rep-resents the relationship between the prompt and the response, but interpreting it as PMI re-quires that pi(ys) approximate (9). One possi-ble alternative is to randomly shuffle prompts in D and average pi(ys|xs′ ) over such nega-tive samples. However, due to computational cost, this paper instead computes pi(ys) using the probability obtained by inputting only the response ys, which may differ from the true PMI. The same limitation also applies to the estimates of mutual information MI (pi).• For (12) to be a good approximation, it is necessary to assume that both pi(y|x)p0(x)

and pi(y)p0(x) are sufficiently close to 

p0(y|x)p0(x). However, if a model pro-duces good responses, then pi(y|x)p0(x) and 

pi(y)p0(x) are generally not close, so this assumption may easily fail. Therefore, the estimate of MI (pi) based on (12) should be regarded only as a rough reference. • The estimated mutual information MI (pi)

measures the strength of association between the prompt x and the response y. However, a strong association between x and y does not necessarily imply that the response is appro-priate.
